Part I

Mathematical foundations

Start with the job a signature has to do. Then build the arithmetic and point operations that make public verification possible.

01 What problem does ECDSA solve?

Alice wants to pay Bob in Bitcoin. She proposes a transaction spending an output locked to a key she controls. Bob and the network need evidence that someone who controls the key authorized this particular spend. If Alice shared the private key, anyone who received it could make a different payment. If she used a password that everyone could check directly, every verifier would know it too.

A digital signature gives Alice a different way to prove control. She generates a private key that stays secret and a related public key she can share. Signing consumes her private key and the message. Checking consumes the public key, the message, and the signature. Both sides must agree on the exact bytes the signature should cover.

  1. Alice preparesChoose the inputs, recipients, and amounts covered by the signature.
  2. Alice signsHash the agreed bytes and use the secret key to produce a signature.
  3. Bob checksRebuild the digest and test the signature with Alice’s public key.
  4. Nodes enforceApply the transaction and spending rules to decide whether to accept it.

Authentication here means evidence of control of a cryptographic key. Connecting that key to a person takes separate evidence. Authorization is the application’s decision that control of that key permits an action. A Bitcoin locking condition can grant that authority over an output. In Ethereum, a transaction signature identifies the sending account and other state rules determine what the account can do.

Encryption protects confidentiality. A signature is intended to be checked by anyone who has the right public inputs, and leaves those inputs readable. A signature should bind the message because otherwise Bob could copy Alice’s signature for “pay 1” onto “pay 10.” A forgery would be a valid signature for a new message under Alice’s established key without the signer signing that message. After working through the example, we’ll see why Bob can check a signature but has a much harder time making one.

Analogy. Think of a publicly checkable seal made with a private stamp. A paper seal can be copied onto another sheet. A digital signature check depends on the contents of the sheet as well as the key.

02 Modular arithmetic from scratch

To do exact algebra on a finite set, we need a rule for staying in that set when we add or multiply. A clock provides the analogy. Seven hours after 10 on a 12-hour clock gives 5. Counting around a whole extra turn changes the elapsed time by 12 while leaving the display unchanged.

For an integer a and a positive modulus m, a mod m is the unique remainder in 0…m−1 after removing whole multiples of m. Thus 23 mod 7 = 2 since 23 = 3 × 7 + 2, and −2 mod 7 = 5 since −2 = −1 × 7 + 5. Some programming languages return a negative remainder for negative inputs. To get the representative used here, add the modulus to a negative remainder.

You can reduce an intermediate result because substituting a + tm for a, with integer t, adds only multiples of m to sums and products. The final remainder survives. That also explains why the exponentiation example can reduce after every multiplication of the base. The exponent itself counts repetitions and follows the period of the group in which the base lives.

What division is trying to undo

To divide by 3 modulo 7, find a residue whose product with 3 is 1. Try 1 through 6: the residues of the products are 3, 6, 2, 5, 1, 4. So 3⁻¹ = 5 mod 7, where the superscript −1 means multiplicative inverse. To evaluate 2 divided by 3, multiply 2 by 5 and reduce to 3. Check it by multiplying 3 by 3, which gives 2 modulo 7. The result lives on the clock.

For larger inputs, use the extended Euclidean algorithm. To invert 7 modulo 17, divide 17 = 2·7 + 3 and 7 = 2·3 + 1. Back-substitute 1 = 7 − 2·3 = 7 − 2(17 − 2·7) = 5·7 − 2·17. The 17 term disappears modulo 17, leaving inverse 5. This works exactly when the greatest common divisor of the value and modulus is 1. Modulo 8, 2 times anything is even, so there is no inverse for 2. With a prime modulus, every nonzero residue is coprime to it and has an inverse. Zero has none.

A finite field is a finite set with well-behaved addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division by nonzero elements. For prime p, the residues 0…p−1 form the field 𝔽ₚ. Our point coordinates use 𝔽₁₇. Later, integers that count generator steps wrap at the separate modulus 19.

Why use modulo almost everywhere? With real or rational coordinates you can still define a group law, but you get a different group. Rationals can grow in bit length and floating-point approximations can lose exact equality. A prime field gives a finite coordinate space, bounded representations, and exact arithmetic with division where needed. A chosen generator gives another finite cycle for scalars. These facts make the construction implementable. Hardness comes from the discrete logarithm problem in a well-chosen large group. Our clock and our 19-point group are simple to search.

03 Ordinary elliptic curves

A curve is a set of solutions to an equation. For a short Weierstrass curve the equation is y² = x³ + ax + b. Here x and y are coordinates, and a and b are public constants that choose the shape. Over the reals this lesson uses a = −1 and b = 1 for simple intersections. The running finite-field curve will use a = b = 2. The coefficients are chosen so the cubic has no repeated root, which avoids singularities such as cusps or crossings.

At x = 0 the real equation gives y² = 1, so both (0, 1) and (0, −1) belong to the curve. Squaring removes the sign, so reflecting any point P = (x, y) across the horizontal axis gives −P = (x, −y). The minus sign here will mean the inverse for point addition.

Vector addition

In the full plane, add coordinates. (−1, 1) plus (0, 1) gives (−1, 2). At x = −1 the cubic is 1, but y² at y = 2 is 4. The result has left the curve.

Curve addition

Stay in the set of solutions. On this curve, the group sum of the same inputs is (1, −1). A horizontal line and a reflection explain why.

Analogy. A game can define a legal move that lands on another playable square. Adding coordinates is a move in the whole plane. The curve needs a move whose result is always another legal curve point.

04 Why draw a line and reflect?

We want a reversible, closed way to combine points so that expressions such as “add this point seven times” have a consistent meaning. A smooth cubic supplies a natural starting observation: once a nonvertical line passes through two known points, substituting the line into the cubic leaves a third root. The group law grew out of studying intersections and rational solutions on cubics. When the curve coefficients and first two points are rational, the third root is rational too, since the sum of the three roots is already determined by the line. The construction works algebraically in the prime field we will use. Stanford notes on the chord-and-tangent law.

On our real curve, the chord through (−1, 1) and (0, 1) is y = 1. Substitution gives x³ − x = x(x−1)(x+1) = 0, so the third intersection is (1, 1). Reflect it to get (1, −1). Clicking through the construction exposes the roles of the line, third root, and reflection.

Geometric construction

Chord and tangent

Why reflection and infinity belong together

Include 𝒪, the point at infinity, as the neutral element. In the projective completion of this Weierstrass curve every vertical line meets the curve at the same point at infinity as well as its finite intersections. Give the three intersections of a line the relation P₁ + P₂ + T = 𝒪, where P₁ and P₂ are the inputs and T is the third intersection. On a vertical line the finite pair is P and its reflection −P. So P + (−P) = 𝒪, and P + 𝒪 = P follows from taking the third intersection on the vertical line through P and 𝒪 and reflecting it back. Consequently the chord sum is −T. Using the third point directly would return −P when combining P with 𝒪, spoiling the identity.

Analogy. Along a level road, a move east and the same move west cancel. Here the vertical line identifies the pair of points that cancel. The identity is a point in the completed mathematical curve, with no finite (x, y) coordinates.

Derive the coordinate formulas

  1. Let the public geometric inputs be P₁ = (x₁,y₁) = (−1,1) and P₂ = (x₂,y₂) = (0,1). For unequal X coordinates, slope is rise over run. Thus λ = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁) = 0/1 = 0. Write the line y = λx + c using intercept c = y₁ − λx₁ = 1.
  2. At an intersection both equations hold. Substitute (λx + c)² = x³ + ax + b, expand and move terms to get x³ − λ²x² + (a − 2λc)x + (b − c²) = 0. For the chord, a = −1 and b = 1, giving x³ − x = 0.
  3. The roots are the X coordinates x₁, x₂, x₃. Expanding (x−x₁)(x−x₂)(x−x₃) shows that its x² coefficient is −(x₁ + x₂ + x₃). Match coefficients to get x₁ + x₂ + x₃ = λ², then x₃ = λ² − x₁ − x₂ = 0 − (−1) − 0 = 1.
  4. The line has height y₁ + λ(x₃ − x₁) at the third root. Negate this height to reflect it. The sum’s Y coordinate is y₃ = λ(x₁ − x₃) − y₁ = 0 − 1 = −1. Reflection leaves x₃ unchanged. These real examples use ordinary division and no modulus.

Why doubling uses a tangent

A chord through a point and itself leaves rise and run both zero. As the second point approaches the first on a smooth branch the chord approaches the tangent. Algebraically the tangent meets the curve twice at the same input, so the input X appears twice among the cubic roots. Differentiate y² = x³ + ax + b with respect to x to get 2y·dy/dx = 3x² + a. At the input the tangent slope is λ = (3x₁² + a)/(2y₁). For (0, 1) it is −1/2, then x₃ = (−1/2)² − 0 − 0 = 1/4 and y₃ = (−1/2)(0 − 1/4) − 1 = −7/8. If y₁ = 0 the point is its own inverse and the tangent is vertical, so doubling gives 𝒪.

Why call it a group law?

Closure comes from the remaining root and reflection, with vertical cases covered by 𝒪. Commutativity follows because switching inputs leaves their line or tangent unchanged. Identity and inverses follow from vertical lines. Associativity is the deeper property: combining three points gives the same result under either parenthesization. The drawing alone does not prove it.

Why associativity has a structural proof

A standard proof represents a point P by the class of the formal difference [P] − [𝒪]. In this construction, sums of zeros minus poles of rational functions count as zero. The ratio of a line through P₁, P₂, T to the line at infinity gives the relation [P₁] + [P₂] + [T] − 3[𝒪] = 0 in those classes, with intersections counted with multiplicity. A vertical line gives the inverse relation. The theorem that each degree-zero class has a unique point representative transfers the associative addition of classes to the points. This is the same line-and-reflection rule viewed through an operation whose associativity is already known. Elliptic Curves lecture notes, group law and divisor-class proof.

05 From a smooth curve to a finite field

Use the same algebra over the public field 𝔽₁₇. With a = b = 2, the relation y² ≡ x³ + 2x + 2 (mod p) and p = 17 defines the running curve. Enumerate the possible X and Y residues from 0 to 16. (5, 1) passes because the left side is 1 and the right side is 137 ≡ 1. (5, 2) fails since 4 and 1 are distinct residues.

Reducing after multiplication changes which pairs solve the equation. Connecting the dots on the finite grid with ordinary line segments has no algebraic meaning. The chord and tangent construction survives because the line equation, polynomial expansion, and coefficient matching only require field operations. The finite formulas use a multiplicative inverse in place of a real fraction.

Point negation keeps x and replaces y with −y mod p. For G = (5, 1), −G = (5, 16). The Y coordinates 1 and 16 add to 17, which is zero in this field. A vertical pair sums to 𝒪. For ordinary distinct points use the chord rule. For two copies use the tangent rule unless the point equals its inverse. If an input is 𝒪 return the other input.

Double G = (5, 1). The tangent numerator 3·5² + 2 = 77 comes from the cubic. The denominator 2·1 = 2 comes from y². Since 2·9 = 18 ≡ 1 modulo 17, the slope is λ = 77·9 ≡ 13. Then x₃ = 13² − 5 − 5 = 159 ≡ 6 and y₃ = 13(5 − 6) − 1 = −14 ≡ 3. The sum is (6, 3).

06 Groups, generators, and scalar multiplication

A group is a set with an operation you can repeat, undo, and regroup. A generator G is a designated starting point for a cycle. From 𝒪, add G repeatedly. The notation 7G means taking seven generator steps. It uses the point law seven times starting at the identity. On our curve G = (5,1) and 7G = (0,6). Multiplying coordinates would give (35, 7), or (1, 7) after coordinate reduction. At x = 1 the cubic is 5 while 7² ≡ 15, so it does not even land on the curve.

Addition by G is reversible by subtracting G. In a finite set the sequence must repeat. If two steps i and j reach the same point, subtract i steps and get back to the starting point after j − i steps. The order n is the smallest positive step count that returns to 𝒪. These reachable points form a cyclic subgroup: sums and inverses stay in that cycle even if the full curve has other points. Our generator has order 19 and reaches every point on the curve.

How to choose between p and n

Ask what the number represents at this step. A field element is a coordinate, a slope, or a number used to compute one. A scalar is a count of how many copies of a point to add, or a number used to compute such a count. In code, imagine these as two types that both happen to be stored as integers.

Coordinates wrap at p = 17

We defined the curve on residues modulo 17. In the tangent for 2G, the denominator is the field element 2, so its inverse is 9 because 2·9 = 18 ≡ 1 (mod 17). The inverse modulo 19 would be 10, but 2·10 ≡ 3 (mod 17). It fails to undo the denominator in the field where the tangent is calculated.

Point counts wrap at n = 19

19G = 𝒪, so adding 19 to the count leaves the point unchanged. For example, 26G = 19G + 7G = 7G = (0, 6). Reducing 26 modulo 17 would give 9 steps and the different point 9G = (7, 6). The point table shows that 17 steps stop at (6, 14).

Analogy. Imagine a path on a game board. The coordinates label squares. The length of the path tells you when you return to your starting square. Here the field has 17 possible values on each axis, and the path through valid points has 19 positions, counting the identity.

For any point P in the generator’s subgroup, write P = jG, where j is its step count. For instance, Q = 7G. Then 19P = 19(jG) = j(19G) = 𝒪. This is why the verifier can reduce coefficients of Q and R modulo 19 too, even when it has no idea what their step counts from G are. Inside any addition or doubling of those points, the slope and coordinate calculations still use modulo 17. RFC 6090 §3, curve operations and subgroup parameters.

Follow the boundary in the running signature. In R = 4G = (3, 1), 4 counts point additions and the coordinates are computed modulo 17. ECDSA then takes the integer representative of the X coordinate and defines r = 3 mod 19. From that moment r is a scalar that can weight Q. The value 3 happens to fit both ranges on this curve. The recovery ID chapter shows why the conversion matters when a field coordinate can be at least n.

Traditional Diffie–Hellman

In a multiplicative group, public base g is combined with itself by multiplication. With sample prime 7 and g = 3, Alice’s secret exponent 2 gives 3² ≡ 2, and Bob’s secret exponent 3 gives 3³ ≡ 6. Alice computes 6² ≡ 1 and Bob computes 2³ ≡ 1. Both got 3⁶ mod 7. This tiny example deliberately wraps to the identity. Real parameters use a suitable large subgroup.

Elliptic-curve Diffie–Hellman

In the additive curve group, Alice’s secret 2 produces 2G and Bob’s 3 produces 3G. Alice computes 2(3G) and Bob computes 3(2G). Both get 6G = (16, 13). ECDH uses this shared point to derive keys. ECDSA uses the same kind of scalar multiplication for signatures.

Both gᵃ mod p and aG mean iterating the group operation a times, where a in this comparison is a scalar, unrelated to the curve coefficient. Reversing either map to find the step count is a discrete logarithm problem in its respective group. For prime-field Diffie–Hellman the exponent period is the order of g, while each residue multiplication uses that example’s field modulus.

Double-and-add exploits the binary representation. To compute 7G, read 7 as 4 + 2 + 1. Double G to get 2G and double 2G to get 4G, then add the three terms. For 6G use 4G + 2G and skip G. A scalar with 256 bits needs a number of group operations proportional to the bit length, even though taking that many single steps is infeasible. Real secret-scalar implementations also address side channels and may use specialized algorithms, precomputation, and projective coordinates.

09 From message to digest

Alice’s intent might be “pay Bob one coin.” The protocol turns structured data into a canonical list of bytes, or serialization, and defines which fields a given signature covers. This matters because a change from a recipient to a different recipient can be one small byte edit. A human-readable label alone leaves ambiguity about what the signer committed to.

  1. Intent“Pay Bob one coin.”
  2. Signing preimageThe exact serialized bytes covered by the application rules.
  3. Hash outputA fixed-length digest of those bytes under the chosen hash function.
  4. Integer zInterpret the designated leftmost digest bits as an unsigned big-endian integer.

A cryptographic hash maps variable-length input to fixed-length output and is designed to make finding collisions or a matching replacement message hard. An ECDSA implementation converts the digest to an integer z. If the digest is longer than the bit length of n, use its leftmost bitlength(n) bits. A shorter digest is interpreted as-is. Subsequent signature arithmetic treats z modulo n. Truncating bits and reducing modulo n are different operations. For example with n = 19, retain five bits. What keeps the key secret

20 Why publishing (r, s) leaves d hidden

Why can Bob verify but not sign for Alice?

Fix Alice’s public key Q = (0, 6) and the message digest z = 8. Imagine Bob has yet to see a signature for it. His task would be to produce a pair that passes under this particular key and digest. Compare the information available when making a pair and when checking a pair:

Making a signature

Bob can pick a nonce count, say k = 4. He can find R = (3, 1) and produce its X residue r = 3. To get s this way he has to solve (4s)G = 8G + 3Q. Alice already knows that Q is 7 steps from G. She can solve 4s ≡ 8 + 3·7 (mod 19) by multiplying by 5. Bob can compute the point 8G + 3Q = (7, 11) from public inputs, but finding its count from G means solving a discrete logarithm. Choosing a nonce gives him the 4 in 4s. It gives him no shortcut to the count on the other side.

Checking a supplied signature

When Alice supplies (3, 12), Bob knows the multiplier 12 and its inverse 8. He can take 8 copies of 8G + 3Q directly to get (3, 1). He then checks X against 3. The point operations use known multipliers, so there is no step count to discover. If someone instead supplies (3, 13) for the same digest and key, the public computation gives (13, 10). Its X residue is 13, so the guess fails.

Choosing a nonce first explains one way the missing secret creates a hard problem. A would-be forger can also start by guessing r and s. Then r affects both the point being computed and the X residue it must match, so running the public check only tests the guess. On the toy curve Bob can look up (7, 11) as 10G, or look up Q as 7G, and produce a signature. On properly implemented secp256k1, finding those step counts is believed to be computationally infeasible. The algebra shows why honest signatures verify and why the obvious signing shortcut requires secret knowledge. It does not prove that every possible forgery method would have to recover d. Anyone who already has Alice’s valid signature can derive its other S form for the same digest and key. Anyone can also generate a key of their own, or use recovery to find a candidate key for a supplied pair. Forgery resistance is about creating a valid signature for a new message under Alice’s existing key. In a real application the verifier derives z from the message being checked. The freely editable z in the toy lab is there to let you inspect the arithmetic.

What the signature reveals about the private key

Anyone can see r and s and usually the digest z and public key Q. They know the curve, the generator, and both moduli. From s ≡ k⁻¹(z + rd) modulo n, multiply by k to get sk ≡ z + rd. Subtract z to get sk − z ≡ rd. Multiply by the inverse of r to get:

d ≡ r⁻¹(sk − z) (mod n)

In the toy example the public values are r = 3, s = 12, z = 8, and n = 19. The formula still needs secret k = 4. The scalar equation by itself allows many solutions when both d and k are unknown. Public Q and reconstructed R impose group equations that determine the scalars, but solving those group equations is the hard part. Reconstructing a point gives coordinates. To recover k you need its step count from G.

The key is hidden in a computational sense. Someone with enough computational power to find a discrete logarithm could recover it, including directly from Q. 22 Two signatures with the same nonce

23 Deterministic signing and other failures

Randomized ECDSA requires an unpredictable, almost-uniform choice in 1…n−1 on every signing. If a random generator restarts with old state, or its output is biased, the nonce distribution can expose information. A source that appears noisy to a human can still be too predictable.

RFC 6979 specifies deterministic nonce derivation using an HMAC-based generator seeded with representations of the private scalar and the hashed message input. HMAC is a keyed hash-based construction. The procedure extracts candidate bits, checks the candidate’s range, and advances its state when it rejects a candidate. The same key, signing input, hash procedure and options give the same result. Different effective inputs produce different derived streams with cryptographically negligible collision chances under the intended assumptions. The stream is secret because it depends on d. This is a reproducible derivation, while a fixed constant k discards the message dependence completely. Some implementations additionally mix extra entropy or use a different approved nonce method. RFC 6979.

Illustrative incident

Documented incident

In August 2013, Bitcoin.org disclosed an Android randomness vulnerability affecting Android wallet software. The advisory links the weakness to insecure wallet keys and describes rotating affected keys after updating. Android security advisory.

Some leaks provide only a fraction of the nonce through timing, cache behavior, electromagnetic measurements, or faulty arithmetic. Across many signatures, partial leaks or bias can constrain the secret enough for specialized mathematical attacks. Constant-time operations and protections against physical side channels matter even when nonce derivation is deterministic. HSM-backed signing can limit access to keys and signing code. Its firmware, randomness, partition policy, and application still influence security. Low-S only selects a signature representative after the nonce-dependent operation. It cannot repair an information leak during signing.

Hints

Worked solution
  1. Key. Double G to get (6, 3) and double again to get (3, 1). Add 4G to 2G to get (16, 13), then add G to get Q = (0, 6). Every tangent, chord, and resulting coordinate uses modulo 17. Key generation walkthrough.
  2. Sign. R = 4G = (3, 1). Convert its X coordinate to r = 3 mod 19. Since 4·5 ≡ 1 (mod 19), s ≡ 5(8 + 3·7) ≡ 12. The signature is (3, 12), with odd nonce-point parity. Both sides of sR = zG + rQ land at 10G since 48 and 29 differ by 19. Signing derivation.
  3. Verify. 12·8 ≡ 1 (mod 19), so w = 8, u₁ = 7, and u₂ = 5. Use G and public coordinates Q to get 7G = (0, 6) and 5Q = (10, 11). Add them modulo 17 to get (3, 1). Then 3 mod 19 matches r. Public verification walkthrough.
  4. Normalize. s′ = 19 − 12 = 7 and −R = (3, −1 mod 17) = (3, 16). The pair (3, 7) has effective parity 0. Its X remains 3, and 7·15 ≡ 12·4 ≡ 10 (mod 19), so the point equation still holds. How the signs cancel.
  5. Recover. At X = 3 the cubic gives 35 ≡ 1 modulo 17. The even root is 16, so the chosen point is 15G. For the normalized signature, s′(15G) − zG = (105 − 8)G = 2G. Since 3·13 ≡ 1 (mod 19), multiplying by 13 gives 26G = 7G = (0, 6). Public key recovery walkthrough.
  6. Secrecy. Alice knows the count d and uses it to compute s. Bob receives s and uses public point multiplication to check it. Reading the counts of Q or R is easy with the toy table and believed computationally infeasible on secp256k1. Checking versus producing a signature.
  7. Reuse. For z₂ = 9, the same nonce gives s₂ ≡ 5(9 + 3·7) ≡ 17. Subtract the signing equations to get (12 − 17)k ≡ 8 − 9. The inverse of −5 modulo 19 is 15, so k ≡ −15 ≡ 4. Then d ≡ 13(12·4 − 8) ≡ 7. The Low-S values are 7 and 2. They give the effective nonce 15 and the same d because both signs flipped. Nonce reuse walkthrough.

Common sticking points

Why does one equation use 17 and the next use 19?

Seventeen says how coordinates wrap and which denominators can be inverted to evaluate the point law. Nineteen says how many additions of G make a full circuit. For example, in Q = dG you can reduce d modulo 19 first, then every chord or tangent uses modulo 17. When ECDSA turns R.x into the scalar r, the integer moves from the coordinate domain into the scalar domain. See what happens when you swap the moduli.

Where does sR = zG + rQ come from?

The verifier can compute the public sum zG + rQ. The signer chooses s to make s copies of R land at that same point. Because R = kG and Q = dG, that means matching the two generator counts sk and z + rd modulo 19. Work through Alice’s counts.

Why can a verifier check (r, s) but struggle to produce it?

Checking starts with both numbers and tests a point computed using known multipliers. Producing s by the signing method starts with a chosen nonce and needs the count d behind the fixed public point Q. Knowing how to find a point from a known count gives no efficient way to read the count from an arbitrary point on secp256k1. Compare the two tasks.

How can drawing a line count as addition?

Addition names an operation with a neutral value, inverses, and associative regrouping. The line supplies a third curve point and reflection gives that point’s inverse. Adding coordinates in the ambient plane can leave the curve. Revisit the geometric construction.

Does the signature reveal R, k, or d?

r publishes an X residue of R. With z and a trusted Q, verification computes the corresponding R. The scalar k is the step count required to reach R, which is a discrete logarithm. The same hard inverse separates Q from d. The scalar signing equation cannot isolate d using the public pair alone because k is still unknown. Revisit key secrecy.

Does ECDSA multiply the message hash by the private key?

The private scalar d is multiplied by r, the temporary point’s X residue. The digest integer z is added to that product. Multiplication by the inverse of k turns the whole sum into s, the multiplier that makes the verification relation hold. Revisit the derivation.

Does Low-S make a signature unique or recover the correct key by itself?

Low-S chooses a representative between s and n−s for a given signature. Different nonces may still yield different signatures. Recovery still needs a digest and a valid choice of nonce point. Its Y bit can differ from the public key’s compression bit, and flips when s changes sign. Revisit the sign change.

Why is reusing an unrevealed nonce different from signing deterministically?

Reusing a nonce across different effective digests supplies two equations with the same unknowns, so subtraction cancels the private-key term. A deterministic generator includes the signing input in its derivation. For the same effective input and signing procedure, repeating one equation adds no new information. Revisit deterministic signing.
