Place a first order

Financial Information eXchange (FIX) is an open standard for exchanging trading messages. It gives independent systems a common vocabulary for instructions like “buy these shares” and events like “40 shares traded.” Here the trading app sends an order, an instruction to buy or sell, to a broker. The broker checks it and can route it to a venue, such as an exchange, where buyers and sellers meet. Firms can also connect directly to a venue. Each connection can have its own identifiers and rules. FIX Trading Community overview.

Our order C42 is a day limit buy for 100 shares of fictional ACME stock. A share is a unit of ownership in a company. The limit of $25.00 is the most we’ll pay per share. The time in force is Day, so any unfilled part expires at the end of the applicable trading session. A market order generally seeks an immediate trade at available prices and carries no specified limit price.

An order book records orders waiting to trade. A bid is an offer to buy, and an offer (also called an ask) is an offer to sell. Here’s the sell side just before C42 arrives.

What if I want the shares right away?

With Immediate or Cancel (59=3), C42 could fill the 40 available now and cancel the remaining 60. With Fill or Kill (59=4), all 100 would have to be available immediately at $25.00 or better. With only 40 available, nothing would trade. Both instructions can use a limit price where supported. The venue’s order types and time-in-force rules determine which combinations are allowed. FIX trade specification, New Order Single.

Session messages

Open, monitor, synchronize, and close the conversation. Examples include Logon and Heartbeat.

Application messages

Carry trading instructions and events. Examples include New Order Single and Execution Report.

A FIX engine typically handles encoding, validation and session mechanics. The trading application decides what orders to send and how to update its order ledger. The matching engine at a venue determines which orders trade.

Why brokers agreed on a shared protocol

FIX began in the early 1990s with electronic equity trading. A fund connected to ten brokers can share its FIX encoder, session engine and core order model across those connections. Each broker still has its own message rules, limits and certification tests. The same approach later expanded to other instruments, market data and the work that follows a trade. Read a FIX message

This lesson uses the TagValue encoding. Each field is a tag=value pair. Start with 35=D. Tag 35 is MsgType, and D means New Order Single. Then find 11=C42 for the client’s order identifier and 34=2 for the message’s place in the sender’s stream.

Read the order from left to right
PartWhat to look for
Header8 version, 9 body length, 35 message type, 49/56 sender/target, 34 sequence number, 52 sending time (UTC).
Body11=C42 client order ID, 55=ACME symbol, 54=1 buy side, 60 transaction time (UTC), 38=100 quantity, 40=2 limit order, 44=25.00 limit price, 59=0 day.

How do you get 126 and 171?

BodyLength counts only the middle segment, starting at 35= and stopping just before 10=. It includes the header fields from 35 onward as well as the order fields. CheckSum sums the byte values of the first two segments, then takes the remainder after dividing by 256 and pads to three digits. Here the sum is 7,083 and the remainder is 171. A printable pipe has a different byte value from SOH.

With 44=26.00, the length stays 126 and the checksum becomes 172. With 44=25.000, the body grows to 127 and the checksum becomes 219 while tag 9 still says 126. To encode an edited message, write the new length first, then calculate the checksum over the updated header and body. FIX TagValue Encoding Standard.

How does the engine find where a message ends?

TCP delivers an ordered stream of bytes. A single read might return half of C42’s message, or all of C42 plus the beginning of the next message. After reading 8 and 9, the engine can wait for the indicated number of bytes and look for the checksum trailer at that boundary. Once it has a complete frame, the engine can validate it and continue with any leftover bytes. For field parsing, some data fields can contain SOH themselves, so the decoder uses their companion length fields. FIX TagValue Encoding Standard.

Why can the same tag appear twice?

A repeating group encodes a list of structured entries. This excerpt from a market data snapshot declares two entries with 268=2. Each starts with 269 and has its own price and size.

268=2|269=0|270=24.80|271=200|269=1|270=24.90|271=40|

Entry one is a bid for 200 at $24.80. Entry two is an offer of 40 at $24.90. A parser needs the message dictionary to recognize the group and entry boundaries. A flat map with one value per tag would lose the bid.

Follow fills and cancels

A single order can produce several messages over time. The broker uses an Execution Report (35=8) to acknowledge the order, report trades and communicate other changes. In these FIX 4.4 examples, ExecType (150) tells you why this report was sent and OrdStatus (39) tells you the reported order state. A new acknowledgement has 150=0 and 39=0. A trade has 150=F, with 39=1 for Partially Filled or 39=2 for Filled in the ordinary fill path.

Which identifier follows what?

11=ClOrdID is assigned by the client to a request. 37=OrderID comes from the broker or venue. 17=ExecID identifies individual fills and many other execution reports. A FIX 4.4 order-status response instead uses 17=0. Identification scope and stability across feeds come from the counterparty spec. 34=MsgSeqNum belongs to one direction of the session and advances across orders and admin messages.

A cancel (35=F) or replace (35=G) gets its own new ClOrdID and uses 41=OrigClOrdID to reference the order being changed. After a successful replace from C42 to C42-R1, a later cancel in this flow references C42-R1. Follow the venue’s rules for overlapping requests and identifier scope.

What if an order fills while a cancel is pending?

In the illustrated race, the cancel carries 11=C42-C1 and 41=C42. The broker reports 150=6 (Pending Cancel). A final 60 shares trade before the cancel takes effect. The trade report can carry 150=F and still show 39=6 because Pending Cancel takes precedence in this flow. A Cancel Reject (35=9) with 39=2 then confirms the order is Filled. An accepted cancel would instead yield an execution report with 150=4 and 39=4. FIX order state matrices, B.1.

What if the price on an earlier fill was wrong?

After C42 fills, suppose the broker corrects the first 40-share trade, E1, from $24.90 to $24.80. The correction has its own report ID (17=E6) and refers to the earlier report with 19=E1 (ExecRefID).

35=8|17=E6|19=E1|150=G|39=2|32=40|31=24.80|14=100|151=0|6=24.92|

150=G means Trade Correct. Update the earlier execution and keep C42 at 100 filled, with the new weighted average (40 × $24.80 + 60 × $25.00) / 100 = $24.92. A Trade Cancel (150=H, also called a bust) reverses a referenced execution. An order cancel closes the quantity still open for trading. Check the counterparty’s rules for how corrections and busts are reported. FIX trade specification, Execution Report.

How do I tell reject messages apart?
  • 35=3 Session Reject can flag a protocol violation, such as an invalid tag value. Look for 45 (reference sequence number), 371 (reference tag) and 373 (session reject reason) when present.
  • 35=8 with 150=8 and 39=8 reports a rejected new order in this model, for example when it fails the broker’s pre-trade checks. Check 103 and 58 when present.
  • 35=9 Order Cancel Reject rejects a cancel or replace request and reports the order’s current status. Check 434 for which request type and 102 for the reason when provided.
  • 35=j Business Message Reject covers application messages for which a more specific rejection mechanism doesn’t apply. Read 372=RefMsgType, 380=BusinessRejectReason, and, when supplied, 45=RefSeqNum and 379=BusinessRejectRefID. For example, 35=j|45=12|372=B|380=3| references sequence 12 and says that the peer doesn’t support application message type B (News). FIX application trade specification.

Recover a missing fill

A FIX session is a numbered conversation between two systems. The initiator (here the trading app) opens a network connection to the acceptor (the broker) and sends Logon (35=A). The broker acknowledges with its own Logon. Once synchronized, either side can send messages, including a fill that arrives minutes after the order. When idle, they use Heartbeat (35=0) at the agreed interval (108). A Test Request (35=1) asks for a heartbeat echoing its 112=TestReqID. Logout (35=5) closes a connection cleanly when possible.

A FIX session can continue on a new TCP connection. Both sides track the next number to send and the next one to expect. They can retain those counters and a store of messages so they can continue after reconnecting. In this example, the broker sent the final fill at sequence 4 and a heartbeat at 5 before the break. The app processed through 3, so it still expects 4. The peers continue with their existing counters.

Step through the reconnect

    Selected message fields. Counters and quantities are from the app’s point of view.

    1. Spot the gap. The app reconnects with Logon 34=3. The broker replies with Logon 34=6. The app sees 6 while expecting 4.
    2. Ask for 4 onward. The app sends 35=2|34=4|7=4|16=0|. The 34=4 is the app’s own sequence, 7=4 starts the range in the broker’s stream, and 16=0 leaves that range open-ended.
    3. Replay the trade. The broker retransmits the report at its original 34=4 with 43=Y (Possible Duplicate) and 122 (original sending time). The app records the previously unseen 60 shares once and now expects 5.
    4. Skip the admin messages in that range. A Sequence Reset in Gap Fill mode (35=4|34=5|43=Y|123=Y|36=7|) covers the old heartbeat at 5 and the Logon already received at 6. The next expected live broker message is 7.

    43=Y means the application may have handled the report before. Establish whether it has already applied the business event using the agreed identifiers and their scope. A count of 100 shares stays 100 if the same trade arrives again. Gap Fill advances the session counter past a skipped range. When a missing business event cannot be recovered from the stream, reconcile through the counterparty’s supported sources. Reset-mode Sequence Reset and a mutually agreed session reset are separate procedures. FIX Session Layer standard.

    What if the connection drops before I get an order acknowledgement?

    Suppose the app sends C42, then disconnects before seeing any response. The order could already be working or filled. Recover the session and check the existing order through the counterparty’s supported status or reconciliation procedure. A fresh buy with a new order ID could leave you with two orders. A disconnect can trigger automatic cancellation on some connections, subject to configured scope and exclusions. NYSE Pillar options for Cancel on Disconnect in Logon.

    If the counterparty supports an application resend, it consumes a new 34 and follows agreed rules for the business identifier and optional 97=Y (PossResend). In a session retransmission, the sender reuses the original 34 with 43=Y. The recovery lab shows the latter. FIX Session Layer, §4.9.

    Read a real venue specification

    A counterparty is the firm or venue on the other side of your FIX link. Its rules of engagement tell you which version and conventions it supports, how to connect, what fields are required and how orders behave. A data dictionary describes allowed messages, field types, values and groups. Workflow rules can go beyond what a dictionary can validate, such as when a request may be retried and how long executions can be recovered.     Where do FIXT.1.1, TLS and FIX Latest fit?

    FIX.4.4 here identifies the session profile and application version. With FIXT.1.1, Logon (8=FIXT.1.1|35=A|…|1137=9, selected fields) declares FIX 5.0 SP2 as the sender’s default application version. A following order can use that default, or use header 1128=6 to select FIX 4.4 where the counterparty allows it. The sequence numbers continue in the same session.

    Extension packs are published updates to the FIX standard. FIXT can describe an extension and a bilateral custom application version with 1156=ApplExtID and 1129=CstmApplVerID, respectively. Logon defaults use their corresponding default fields, 1407 and 1408, where supported. Obtain the exact venue profile and dictionary rather than inferring supported fields from 8. FIX Session and FIXT specifications · FIX extension packs.

    TLS protects the network link. FIX-over-TLS (FIXS) describes interoperable ways to secure it. With mutual TLS, both peers authenticate with certificates. FIX user authentication (FIXA) authenticates the client within the protected FIX session in supported profiles. Use your counterparty’s test endpoints and credential rules and redact Logon secrets from shared traces. FIX-over-TLS standard.

    Explore other FIX workflows

    Order entry tells you what happened to your order. The same protocol family can also describe the market around the order, a dealer’s price for a proposed trade, or what happens after execution. Counterparties often put these workflows on separate interfaces with separate state and recovery rules.

    Matching · Which orders trade first?

    A central limit order book keeps resting buy and sell orders for each instrument. In this price–time example, the highest bid and lowest offer have priority. Among orders at the same price, the earlier one goes first. An incoming buy can cross an offer at or below its limit. An incoming sell can cross a bid at or above its limit. Each trade has size min(buy open, sell open) and uses the resting order’s price in this example.

    Drop copy and post-trade · What gets booked?

    Drop copy is a separate stream that can deliver copies of order or execution events to risk and reconciliation systems. NYSE Pillar can deliver copies of activity from its binary order gateway over a FIX interface. The configured scope can cover fills only or a broader set of order events. Compare the primary and drop-copy event IDs in their agreed scopes. If the sides disagree, investigate the source of the missing or duplicated event before using the aggregate position. NYSE Pillar Binary Gateway specification, §7.4.

    A Trade Capture Report (AE) describes a trade, whereas an Execution Report can also acknowledge or update a working order. Under a supported profile, a Trade Capture Report Request (AD) can request historical or streaming trade records. For C42, reconcile reports TR1 (40 at $24.90) and TR2 (60 at $25.00) to the 100 shares and $2,496 traded value before allocating.

    1. Allocate. An Allocation Instruction (J) with 70=A42 splits 100 shares using an account group (78=2). Fund A gets 70 and Fund B gets 30. An instruction can also specify allocations before execution under some profiles. Link the order and execution IDs so two similar blocks don’t get confused.
    2. Check the booking response. An Allocation Instruction Ack (P) can say received, accepted or rejected through 87 and explain failures through 88. An Allocation Report (AS) can send account detail, including 153 for the allocation’s average price and 154 for the account’s net cash amount. Its 755=AllocReportID is the report’s own ID. A separate AT can acknowledge the report.
    3. Confirm each account. The broker sends AK with 664=ConfirmID, the allocation reference, account, quantity, price and cash amounts. With a fee of $0.01 per share, Fund A’s gross $1,747.20 becomes $1,747.90 net. Fund B’s gross $748.80 becomes $749.10 net. In AU, tag 940=3 affirms and 940=2 rejects. If Fund B rejects an incorrect account, a revised AK links back with 772=ConfirmRefID.
    4. Reconcile positions. An AN positions request can lead to an AP report. Match the request with 710 and check the clearing business date (715), account and instrument. Read long and short quantities (704/705) in the 702 position group. This example ends at Fund A long 70 and Fund B long 30. Settlement is the later movement of securities and cash. A rejected or unconfirmed allocation can still represent economic exposure before it is ready to settle.

    Other post-trade profiles cover standing settlement instructions, settlement status, registrations, collateral, margin, account reporting, adjustments and cash movements. Their exact workflows vary by asset and participants. FIX post-trade workflows · Run the post-trade lab.

    Risk, status and routing · What should the app do next?

    Pre-trade risk is the set of checks before an order is released. C42’s maximum limit notional is 100 × $25.00 = $2,500. A broker with a $2,000 order limit rejects it before it enters the book. Other checks cover size, prices outside a collar around a fresh reference, aggregate credit, position plus working exposure, and daily traded volume. Checks may run at the client, broker and venue. A kill switch first blocks further admissions for its scope. It can then request cancellation and verify working orders actually closed. Previously executed trades still stand.

    A smart order router chooses where to place an order. Suppose A shows 70 shares at $24.99 plus $0.04 fee, and B shows 60 at $25.00 plus $0.01. For a 100-share buy with a $25.05 limit in this toy market, route 60 to B at effective $25.01 and 40 to A at effective $25.03. If B only fills 40, reserve the 20 still working there until its outcome is confirmed. A stale quote or uncertain cancel changes what is safe to route.

    An execution algorithm controls how a parent order is worked over time. A simple time-weighted strategy can release four 25-share orders at 0, 15, 30 and 45 seconds.     Try each prompt before opening the answer. The fields are selected excerpts from the walkthroughs.

    35=8|150=0|39=0|14=0|151=100| Did anything trade?

    No. The New execution report acknowledges that the order is working. Filled quantity is 0 and 100 shares can still trade.

    C42 filled 40 shares. A replace asks for total quantity 80 and succeeds. How many are open?

    40 shares. In this example the replacement quantity is the new total. The 40 already filled remain part of that total. Check the replace acknowledgement before updating the order’s working size.

    Your app expects 34=4 from the broker. Its own next outgoing number is 11. The broker sends 6. Which numbers go in an open-ended resend request?

    34=11 is the app’s next outgoing sequence. 7=4|16=0| asks for messages from 4 onward in the broker’s separate stream. A finite request for just the missing range would use 16=5 where supported.

    The last report says Filled and arrives again with 43=Y. What do you check?

    Check whether the business event was already applied, according to the counterparty’s execution identifiers and your ledger. If it was, quantities stay the same. If the application never applied it, process the recovered event according to that workflow.

    Investigate a stale order screen

    In this variation of the reconnect, the FIX engine reports that recovery finished. The order screen still shows 40 filled. Compare three pieces of evidence from the same session.

    Engine event logRecovery complete. Next broker sequence is 7.
    Application ledgerC42 has 40 filled. Last applied execution is E1.
    What does the broker report, and what would you check next?
    1. Identify the order and event. 11=C42 and 37=B510 tie the report to the order. 17=E2 identifies the report. 32=60, 14=100 and 151=0 say the last 60 traded, for 100 filled and zero open.
    2. Check both timelines. The engine advanced to 7 after the gap. The application ledger still shows E1. 43=Y marks E2 as a replay at its original sequence 4. Tag 122 gives the original sending time, one minute earlier than the replay’s 52.
    3. Trace E2 into the ledger. Check validation, delivery to the order handler, duplicate detection and whether the write committed. If E2 was applied, check how the screen reads that state. Confirm the broker’s current order and execution state through the agreed reconciliation channel before repairing local state or deciding whether another order is needed.
